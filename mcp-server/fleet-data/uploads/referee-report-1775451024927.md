# Referee Report: "The Balancing Act in Causal Inference"

## Summary

This paper provides a synthesis of the balancing weights literature for causal inference, unifying the "modeling" and "balancing" approaches to inverse propensity weighting under a common framework based on maximal imbalance and its connection to empirical process theory. The paper covers the IPW and AIPW estimators, models for balance, the degree-of-balance trade-off (including connections to nonparametric regression theory via symmetrization and the modulus of continuity), extensions to general estimands via Riesz representers, and a novel treatment of matching via discrepancy-theoretic rounding. The paper targets the Biometrika synthesis paper format.

## Overall Assessment

This is an ambitious and largely successful synthesis. The paper's central insight --- that the empirical process quantities governing nonparametric regression (Rademacher complexity, entropy integrals, the fixed-point radius) are *the same* quantities governing balance --- is clearly articulated and well-developed. The matching-via-rounding section (Section 7) contains genuinely original contributions that go well beyond synthesis.

**Main strengths:**

- The unifying thread through maximal imbalance is effective. It gives the reader a single object to track from IPW through AIPW, model choice, asymptotic theory, and matching.
- The connection between balance and regression complexity (Section 5) is the paper's intellectual core and is handled with real care. The symmetrization walkthrough, the "why $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ is not enough" discussion, and the modulus-of-continuity perspective are valuable and clearly written.
- The matching section is a genuine contribution. The use of hereditary discrepancy to bound rounding error, the sharp characterization of the variance cost of matching, and the feasibility result for integer-constrained balancing weights are new and interesting.
- The paper is honest about what remains open (e.g., automatic selection of the tuning parameter $\sigma^2$, sparse weighting as an alternative to matching).

**Main weaknesses:**

- The paper is very long and uneven in depth. Sections 2--3 are relatively standard and could be compressed; Sections 5 and 7 (plus appendices) are dense and technical, creating a mismatch in the audience the paper seems to address.
- The paper has no simulation study or data analysis. For a synthesis paper aimed at bridging theory and practice, this is a notable omission.
- Some important related work receives insufficient attention.
- The transition from synthesis to original contribution is not clearly signposted.

## Major Comments

### 1. Length and balance across sections

The paper runs well over the synthesis track's 31-page main body limit (even before appendices). More importantly, the depth varies dramatically. Sections 2--3 (Framework, Estimating IPW) cover well-trodden ground and could be shortened by 30--40% without loss. The models section (Section 4) spends substantial space on binary covariates and basis expansions that most readers in this literature will know. Meanwhile, Section 5 (Degree of Balance) and Section 7 (Matching) contain the paper's most original and technically demanding material.

This creates a readership problem. The reader who benefits most from Sections 2--4 is unlikely to follow the empirical process arguments in Section 5 or the discrepancy theory in Section 7. I would recommend the authors either (a) compress the expository sections and be explicit that Section 5 onward assumes more technical background, or (b) move the most technical subsections (e.g., the symmetrization walkthrough, the full modulus-of-continuity development, the discrepancy proofs) to the supplement, leaving clear statements and intuitive discussion in the main text.

### 2. Missing empirical content

A synthesis paper in Biometrika should demonstrate the ideas it unifies. The paper has two illustrative figures (the geometry figure and the in-sample vs. out-of-sample balance figure) but no simulation comparing methods and no data application. At minimum, I would expect:

- A simulation comparing IPW, balancing weights, and AIPW under varying model complexity and overlap conditions, illustrating the degree-of-balance trade-off from Section 5.
- Ideally, a data example showing how the choice of model $\mathcal{M}$ affects balance and inference in practice.

The figures that do appear (Figures 1--2) are well-conceived but serve more as visual aids for the theory than as empirical demonstrations. Figure 1 in particular is dense and would benefit from more discussion in the main text.

### 3. Relationship to the double/debiased machine learning literature

The paper acknowledges the cross-fitting/DML literature (Chernozhukov et al., 2018) but does not engage with it deeply. In particular:

- The paper's argument for why balancing weights can avoid cross-fitting (the "in-sample advantage" of Section 5.3, illustrated in Figure 2) deserves more discussion. This is a genuinely interesting claim --- that in-sample balancing exploits structure that cross-fitting discards --- but the implications for practice are not drawn out. When should a practitioner use balancing weights without cross-fitting vs. cross-fitted DML?
- The Neyman orthogonality / debiased ML framework provides an alternative unifying perspective on AIPW that some readers will find more natural. The paper should acknowledge this more explicitly and explain how its maximal-imbalance perspective relates to or improves upon it.

### 4. The matching section needs clearer motivation and scope

Section 7 is the paper's most original section but it arrives somewhat abruptly. The transition from "degree of balance" (Section 5) to "other estimands" (Section 6) to "matching" (Section 7) lacks a clear narrative arc. Why does matching appear after general estimands rather than after the balance trade-off discussion? The connection between rounding and the balance theory developed earlier should be made more explicit at the start of Section 7.

Additionally, the matching results are stated for the ATT estimand, while the rest of the paper focuses on the ATE. The reason for this switch should be explained earlier (it appears only implicitly --- matching is more natural for ATT because control weights are the ones being rounded).

### 5. The paper should discuss practical guidance more concretely

The paper develops a rich theoretical framework but offers limited practical guidance. Key questions a practitioner would have:

- How do I choose the model $\mathcal{M}$ in practice? The Sobolev space discussion is theoretically clean but requires choosing smoothness parameters that practitioners rarely have intuition about.
- How do I choose $\sigma^2$? The paper acknowledges this is open (Section 4.3) but could say more about what existing approaches do and when they work.
- When should I use balancing weights vs. propensity score modeling vs. DML? The paper's framework should allow some concrete recommendations, even if hedged.

A short "practical recommendations" subsection would significantly increase the paper's impact.

## Minor Comments

### Notation and exposition

1. **Equation (5), the maximal imbalance definition**: The notation $\text{imbalance}\_{\mathcal{M}}(\hat\gamma)$ is used throughout but is somewhat heavy. Consider a shorter notation (e.g., $\mathcal{I}\_\mathcal{M}$) to reduce visual clutter in the many equations that follow.

2. **Section 2.3, the AIPW error decomposition (Equation 8)**: The paper uses $\delta m$ and $\delta\mu$ seemingly interchangeably for the regression error $\hat\mu_1 - \mu_1$. In Equation (8) and surrounding text, $\delta m$ is defined but then $\delta\mu_1$ also appears. This should be unified.

3. **Remark 1 (TMLE)**: This remark is well-placed and informative, but the last sentence ("For the purpose of our discussion below, this *is* an AIPW estimator") could be more precise about in what sense. TMLE and AIPW have different finite-sample behavior; the equivalence is asymptotic.

4. **Section 3.2, the dual characterization (Equation 11)**: The "mean zero noise" term in the dual is presented without much explanation of why it is noise-like. A sentence noting that it is an empirical process term centered at zero by the balance property would help.

5. **Proposition 1**: The condition that "each squared weight is negligible compared to their sum $n\Gamma^2$" is an infinitesimality condition that should be named as such (it is, later, in the appendix version). The body statement would benefit from naming it.

6. **Section 4.2.2 (Function space perspective)**: The Sobolev norm equations (13)--(16) are presented for functions on $[0,1]^2$. This is fine for illustration, but the reader should be told more explicitly that the ideas extend to $[0,1]^p$ and that the choice of $p=2$ is purely for notational convenience.

7. **Section 5.1, Equation (20)**: The symmetrized imbalance uses Gaussian multipliers $\xi_i$, but the footnote mentions Rademacher multipliers as well. The relationship between these two --- that they give equivalent complexity measures up to constant factors --- could be stated more prominently, as many readers will be more familiar with the Rademacher version.

8. **Section 5.3 (Doing Better)**: The modulus of continuity is introduced via Equation (26) in its penalized form, with the constrained form deferred to Appendix A. For readers familiar with the Donoho-Liu framework, the constrained form is more natural. Consider presenting both forms together, or at least noting that the constrained form $\bar\omega(\delta)$ appears in the appendix.

9. **Section 6 (Other estimands)**: The generalization to Riesz representers is clean but compressed. The paper jumps from the specific ($\psi_1$) to the very general (any linear functional of $m$) in one paragraph. An intermediate example --- e.g., working through the ATT or the dose-response curve explicitly --- would help readers who are not already familiar with the Riesz representer framework.

10. **Section 7, Equation (35)**: The notation $\overset{\neg}{W}\_i$ for $1 - W_i$ is nonstandard and visually hard to parse. Consider using $(1-W_i)$ directly or defining a cleaner shorthand.

11. **Remark on nearest-neighbor matching**: The claim that nearest-neighbor matching is the $\sigma^2 = 0$ case of minimax weights for the Lipschitz model is striking and deserves a forward reference to Kallus (2020) earlier in the paper, perhaps when first discussing the modeling vs. balancing distinction.

12. **Appendix C (Rounding)**: The proof of Lemma 1 is long and technical, occupying several pages. While thorough, it may deter readers. Consider whether a proof sketch in the appendix with full details in a supplementary file would be more appropriate for the Biometrika format.

13. **Citations**: The paper cites "Robins et al. (1994)" for AIPW but also attributes AIPW to other sources in different places. The attribution should be consistent. Also, the survey by Chattopadhyay and Zubizarreta (2023, Statistical Science) on balancing weights covers overlapping ground and should be cited and discussed.

14. **Bracket conventions**: The paper mostly follows Biometrika's bracket nesting convention $[\{()\}]$ but there are a few places where $\text{E}[\cdot]$ appears inside parentheses rather than switching to $\text{E}(\cdot)$ (or vice versa). A careful pass for bracket consistency would be worthwhile.

### Typographical and minor issues

15. The paper switches between $\text{var}$ and $\text{Var}$ in a few places (e.g., the Biometrika convention calls for lowercase $\text{var}$).

16. In the abstract, the phrase "essentially by the method of moments" is vague. Consider "as a method of moments estimator defined by the sample balance conditions."

17. Footnote 1 (design-based weights): This footnote is long and could be shortened or moved to the main text. The point that weights are functions of $X_i$ rather than indexed by $i$ is worth making prominently.

18. The paper uses $\tilde\psi_1$ for the sample analog of $\psi_1$. The distinction between $\psi_1$ (population) and $\tilde\psi_1$ (sample) should be signposted more clearly when it first appears, as the switch happens mid-discussion in Section 4.

## Recommendation

This paper makes a genuine contribution to the causal inference literature. The unifying framework is valuable, the connections to empirical process theory are illuminating, and the matching results are novel. However, the paper needs revision to address the length/balance issue, the lack of empirical content, and several expository issues. I recommend **major revision**, with particular attention to:

1. Compressing Sections 2--4 and/or moving technical details from Sections 5 and 7 to the supplement.
2. Adding at least one simulation study demonstrating the key theoretical insights.
3. Providing clearer practical guidance.
4. Improving the narrative flow, especially the transition to matching.
5. Engaging more deeply with the DML literature and recent surveys on balancing weights.
