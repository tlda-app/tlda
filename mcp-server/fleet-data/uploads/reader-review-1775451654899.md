# Reader Review: The Balancing Act in Causal Inference

Reading as an applied statistician/econometrician who knows IPW and AIPW basics and wants to understand balancing weights deeply enough to make good methodological choices.

---

## 1. Where the Argument Lost Me

### Section 2.2 (Imbalance and estimation error): the model $\mathcal{M}$ appears without preparation

The paper introduces the idea of bounding bias via a "model" $\mathcal{M}$ (Equation 6) almost in passing: "One natural approach is to think of $\mu_1$ as belonging to some set of functions $\mathcal{M}$." This is the single most important concept in the paper---everything that follows depends on it---but it arrives as a one-sentence aside. I had to re-read several times before I understood that $\mathcal{M}$ is not a parametric model for the propensity score or outcome, but a set of candidate functions whose geometry determines the maximal imbalance. A sentence or two making this more concrete at the outset ("for example, $\mathcal{M}$ might be the set of all linear functions of the covariates, in which case...") would have helped enormously.

### Section 3.2 (Balancing approach): the dual characterization (Equation 13) is hard to parse

The dual least-squares characterization in Equation 13 is introduced and then the reader is told the middle term has "mean zero." Why? I eventually figured out that this follows from the population balance property, but on first reading it felt like a rabbit from a hat. More importantly, the *point* of the dual characterization---that balancing and propensity score modeling are two views of the same problem---gets somewhat buried in notation. I would have benefited from a single sentence saying "this means that minimizing imbalance subject to a penalty is equivalent to fitting a penalized regression for the inverse propensity score."

### Section 3.2: the general case (Equation 11) and the index function $g(x)$

The paragraph beginning "The general case has a qualitatively similar dual characterization" lost me. It introduces an index function $g(x)$, a link function $\eta_\chi$, and a parameterization $\gamma(X_i) = \eta_\chi\{g(X_i)\}$ in rapid succession, without examples or a clear statement of why I should care. The subsequent sentence about convergence of $\hat{g}_\chi$ to $g_\chi = \eta_\chi^{-1} \circ \gamma^{\text{ipw}}$ is technically precise but opaque on first reading. I understand this is a bridge to existing literature, but as a reader I wasn't sure whether this was a key insight or a technical aside I could skip.

### Section 5 (Degree of balance): the shift from "plug-in comparison" to "why $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ is not enough" to augmentation

Section 5 is the technical heart of the paper, and the logic is genuinely illuminating once you see it. But the first time through, I lost the thread around "Why $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ is not enough." The argument proceeds: (1) plug in the true weights, the maximal imbalance is $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ for Donsker models, (2) but $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ is not $o_p(n^{-1/2})$, (3) so we need augmentation, which gives us a shrinking model, (4) and equicontinuity converts $O_p$ into $o_p$ on the shrinking model. The problem is that step (2) arrives as a negative result---"this is not enough"---without a clear signpost that the resolution is coming in step (3). I kept re-reading step (2) trying to figure out if I'd missed something, rather than reading on.

### Section 5.3 (Doing better than the true weights): the modulus of continuity

The modulus of continuity $\omega(s)$ in Equation 28 appeared and I genuinely did not know what the variable $s$ represented, what the supremum was over (functions in $\mathcal{M}$ --- but what is the penalty term doing?), or how to interpret it. The subsequent claim that "when $\omega$ is approximately linear, maximal imbalance is approximately zero" (Equation 29) is stated without much intuition. I eventually worked out that $s$ parameterizes a bias-variance tradeoff and $\omega(s) - \omega'(s)s$ is the intercept of the tangent line, but this took several readings. This is the point in the paper where I felt most strongly that I was missing prerequisite knowledge. The connection back to the fixed-point inequality (which *was* clear) helps, but it arrives a paragraph later.

### Section 5.3: the approximation condition for Proposition 2

The condition "there must exist a function $g$ with $\|g\|_{\mathcal{M}} \le C r^2 n / \sigma^2$ and $\|g - \gamma^{\text{ipw}}\|_{L_2(P)} \le C r^2 \sqrt{n}/\sigma$" is stated inline rather than as a displayed condition, and depends on four quantities ($g$, $r$, $n$, $\sigma$) in a way that I found hard to hold in my head. It is also unclear on first reading what role $\sigma$ plays here---is it the same $\sigma$ as the tuning parameter from Equation 9? (I believe it is, but the text doesn't say so explicitly.)

### Section 7 (Matching): the transition from continuous weights to integer weights via discrepancy theory

The rounding section is ambitious and interesting. But the transition from Bernoulli rounding (which is easy to understand) to the hereditary discrepancy argument (which requires combinatorics background) was abrupt. The intuition paragraph about "coloring" is helpful, but it comes after the formal definitions, so I had already been lost for a paragraph before the intuition arrived. The two caveats paragraph is also confusing: it raises the issue that discrepancy is defined in terms of sums rather than averages, and that models containing all constants have infinite hereditary discrepancy, but doesn't resolve these issues clearly until later.

## 2. Where Notation Tripped Me Up

### $\delta m$ vs. $\delta \mu_1$ vs. $\dm$

The paper uses $\dm$ (from the macro `\dm`, which expands to $\delta \mu$) to denote the regression error $\hat{\mu}_1 - \mu_1$, but also uses $\delta m$ in some places and $\delta \mu_1$ in others. In the error decomposition (Equation 8), the text says "in terms of $\varepsilon_i := Y_i - \mu_{W_i}(X_i)$" and later refers to "the regression error function $\delta \mu_1 = \mu_1 - \hat{\mu}_1$." But shortly after, in the AIPW error decomposition, the sign convention appears to flip: "imbalance in $\dm$" where $\dm = \hat{\mu}_1 - \mu_1$. I had to check multiple times whether $\dm$ was $\hat{\mu}_1 - \mu_1$ or $\mu_1 - \hat{\mu}_1$.

### $\gamma^{\text{ipw}}$ vs. $\hat{\gamma}$ vs. $\gamma$

Early on, $\gamma^{\text{ipw}}(x) = 1/e(x)$ is the true inverse propensity weight, $\hat{\gamma}$ is an estimate, and $\gamma$ without a hat or superscript appears in optimization problems as the decision variable. This is consistent but requires the reader to carefully track hats and superscripts. In Equation 13, the notation $\gamma$ appears as the argument of the minimization *and* appears in $\gamma^{\text{ipw}}$ inside the objective function. This is correct but visually confusing.

### $\model$ vs. $\model_0$ vs. $\model_n$

In Section 5.2, the paper introduces $\model_0$ (a priori model), $\model_n$ (shrinking model incorporating the regression error bound), and $\model$ (which is sometimes the general model and sometimes $\model_0$). The sentence "they show that it works well enough to simply use the model $\model_0$ instead" left me briefly confused about which model was being replaced.

### The general Riesz representer notation in Section 6

In Section 6, $\gamma_\psi(w, x)$ becomes the Riesz representer, $h(W_i, X_i, m)$ replaces $\mu_1(X_i)$, and $m(W_i, X_i)$ replaces $\mu_{W_i}(X_i)$. The substitution instructions ("substitute $\gamma_\psi(W_i, X_i)$ for $W_i \gamma^{\text{ipw}}(X_i)$ and then $h(W_i, X_i, m)$ for $\mu_1(X_i)$") are helpful, but the transition is fast. When I reached Proposition 2 (the general version), the notation had shifted enough that I had to go back and re-derive the correspondence.

### $\theta$ vs. $\hat{\theta}$ vs. $\lfloor\hat{\theta}\rceil$ in Section 7

In the matching section, $\theta_i$ denotes the rescaled weights $\gamma_i / \bar{W}$, and $\hat{\theta}$ is their estimate. Then $\lfloor\hat{\theta}\rceil$ is the rounded version, and $\hat{r}_i$ is the fractional part. With $\overset{\neg}{W}_i$ for $1-W_i$, the formulas become quite dense. This is largely unavoidable given what's being said, but the notation overhead is high.

## 3. Where I Lost the Thread

### The Sobolev space discussion in Section 4.2.2

The Sobolev norms (Equations 17--20) and the Remark on invariance are clearly written, but by the time I finished them, I had forgotten *why* I was reading about Sobolev spaces. The connection back to the practical question---"what model should I use for my data?"---gets lost in the mathematics of derivatives and Fourier coefficients. A grounding sentence like "in practice, choosing a Sobolev model amounts to specifying how smooth you believe the conditional mean outcome is" at the start or end of the subsection would have helped.

### The symmetrization argument in Section 5.1

The symmetrization paragraph ("Symmetrization and the connection to regression") is one of the most technically beautiful parts of the paper, but it is also where I most completely lost the thread of *why* I was reading it. The point---that the same notion of model complexity governs both nonparametric regression and balance---is profound, but it arrives at the end of a fairly involved detour through least squares error decompositions. The fixed-point argument (Equation 22) requires effort, and the connection to balance (the sentence beginning "The right-hand side is exactly the same...") comes only after the least-squares material has been fully developed. I would have appreciated a forward pointer at the start: "we develop this connection to regression because it gives us the right tools to analyze balance."

### Other estimands (Section 6)

By the time I reached Section 6, I had internalized the framework for the treatment-specific mean and was ready to see how it generalizes. The generalization itself is clean, but the parade of examples (dose-response, population-targeted ATE, CATE as a limiting case, IV, quantile treatment effects) in rapid succession made it hard to absorb any one of them. I'm not sure what the fix is---perhaps this is inherent in a synthesis paper---but I found myself skimming rather than reading by the end of this section.

## 4. What Clicked

### The error decomposition (Equations 7--8)

The decomposition of the IPW error into imbalance + noise + sampling variation is crystal clear and immediately useful. The observation that "the estimator's design-conditional bias *is* the imbalance in $\mu_1$" is the kind of clean identification that makes the rest of the paper possible. This is where I first felt like the paper was teaching me something I couldn't get elsewhere.

### The bias decomposition for AIPW (Equation 8)

The parallel decomposition for AIPW, where imbalance in $\mu_1$ is replaced by imbalance in the regression error $\delta m$, is elegant and immediately conveys why augmentation helps. The sentence "Intuitively, imbalance with augmentation should be smaller because the regression error function $\delta m$ should be smaller than $\mu_1$ itself" is perfect.

### The binary covariates progression (Section 4.1)

The progression from no interactions to strong interactions to weak interactions in Section 4.1 is pedagogically excellent. Starting with binary covariates makes the abstract machinery of models and imbalance measures completely concrete. The connection to raking and post-stratification anchors the abstract framework in things I already know.

### The "Why $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ is not enough" example

Despite my complaint above about losing the thread, the single-function example ($\mathcal{M} = \{\mu_1\}$) that shows $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ imbalance even with the true weights is a genuine "aha" moment. It makes vivid why we need augmentation or equicontinuity, not just good weights.

### The variance comparison in Section 4 (asymptotic behavior)

The $a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)^2 + 2(a-b)b$ decomposition showing that you can't beat the inverse propensity weights' variance without sacrificing balance is clean and surprising. It addresses a misconception I've encountered in practice (people thinking balancing weights have lower variance than IPW).

### Figure 2 (in-sample vs. out-of-sample balance)

This figure makes the in-sample advantage of balancing weights visually immediate. The three-regime structure (undersmoothing / bias-variance / constant weights) is clear and the fact that in-sample imbalance monotonically decreases while out-of-sample imbalance bottoms out and rises is exactly the right visualization.

### The rounding-to-matching bridge

The idea that any good continuous weights can be rounded to integers with negligible change in imbalance is a clean, surprising result. The Bernoulli rounding setup makes the cost of matching concrete: it is the variance of the rounding, which is bounded by the hereditary discrepancy.

## 5. Questions the Paper Left Me With

### How do I choose a model in practice?

The paper gives an excellent theoretical framework for thinking about models (binary covariates with interactions, Sobolev spaces, RKHSes), but I finished without a clear sense of how to make this choice for a real dataset. If I have 20 continuous covariates and 500 observations, what model should I use? The paper acknowledges this is hard ("the subject of nonparametric models is vast") but doesn't offer practical guidance.

### What happens with poor overlap?

The paper focuses on the sufficient overlap case ($\E[1/e(X)] < \infty$) and mentions that things get harder without it. But many real observational studies have regions of near-zero overlap. How do balancing weights behave in that regime? The root-mean-squared weight $\Gamma$ captures this to some extent, but I left wanting more discussion of what practitioners should do when overlap is questionable.

### How does augmentation interact with model choice in practice?

The paper shows that for convex models, augmentation is unnecessary for near-optimality (Remark 6), and that for nonconvex models like sparse models, augmentation can cut RMSE by half. But how should this guide my choice of $\hat{\mu}_1$ in practice? Should I always augment? Is there a cost to augmenting with a bad $\hat{\mu}_1$?

### What is the practical significance of the matching cost (Equation 34)?

The matching cost formula $r(x)\{1-r(x)\} v_0(x)$ is elegant, but I left unsure about its practical magnitude. Is it typically small? Large? How does it compare to the variance of the unrounded estimator? The remark about comparing to IPW on a subsample helps, but a worked example or simulation would have made this concrete.

### How should I choose $\sigma^2$?

The paper says this is an open question and that some approaches have been proposed but evidence is limited. That's honest, but it leaves me with a practical gap. The minimax interpretation suggests $\sigma^2 \approx \Var[Y \mid X, W]$, but this requires knowing something I'm trying to estimate.

### What is the relationship between the modulus-of-continuity route (Section 5.3) and the inner-product route (Section 5.4)?

Both analyze the bias of AIPW, but they seem to operate at different levels of generality. The modulus route works with convex models and bounds imbalance directly from model geometry. The inner-product route decomposes bias into a product of errors and can exploit instance-specific structure. Are these complementary tools for the same problem? Does one subsume the other in some regime?
